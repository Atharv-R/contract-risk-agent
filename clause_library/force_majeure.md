# Force Majeure Clauses

## What It Is
Force majeure clauses excuse or delay performance when extraordinary events beyond a party's control make performance impossible or impracticable. Common triggers include natural disasters, wars, pandemics, and government actions.

## Standard and Acceptable
- Applies equally to both parties
- Reasonable list of triggering events including natural disasters, war, government actions
- Explicit inclusion of pandemics and epidemics (post-COVID standard)
- Obligation to provide prompt notice of the force majeure event
- Duty to mitigate and resume performance as soon as reasonably possible
- Right to terminate if force majeure continues beyond a reasonable period (90-180 days)
- Payment obligations may be suspended during force majeure
- Affected party must demonstrate the causal connection between the event and non-performance

## Red Flags
- Force majeure clause is entirely absent from the contract
- Only one party can invoke force majeure
- Extremely narrow list of qualifying events
- No pandemic or epidemic coverage
- No right to terminate if force majeure persists indefinitely
- Payment obligations continue even during force majeure
- No notice requirement (allows retroactive claims)
- Includes foreseeable business risks that should be managed (e.g., supply chain fluctuations, market changes)
- Economic hardship or change in market conditions included as force majeure (too broad)

## Example: Acceptable Language
"Neither party shall be liable for delays or failure to perform due to causes beyond its reasonable control, including natural disasters, pandemic, war, terrorism, government actions, or infrastructure failure. The affected party shall provide notice within 5 business days. Either party may terminate if force majeure continues for more than 120 days."

## Example: Problematic Language
No force majeure clause present in the agreement, or: "Company shall be excused from performance obligations due to any circumstances Company deems beyond its control. Service Provider shall not be entitled to invoke force majeure and must perform regardless of circumstances."

## Jurisdiction Notes
- Civil law jurisdictions (France, Germany) may imply force majeure even without an express clause
- Common law jurisdictions (US, UK) generally require an express force majeure clause
- The doctrine of frustration or impracticability may apply in absence of a force majeure clause (UCC 2-615)
- COVID-19 litigation has clarified that pandemics can qualify as force majeure if explicitly included
